# The Book of Jakpa

## I. The Chase

The dust rose before the horses did.

Two mares came tearing across the yellow grassland at the hour when the sun sits low and mean, and on their backs rode two young women who had not slept in three days. Asana rode ahead. Her wrapper was knotted twice around her waist and once around her thigh so it would not catch the wind, and her braids had come loose and streamed behind her like smoke off a burning roof. Yidana rode half a length back, low over her horse's neck, her mouth open, her breath torn out of her the moment she found it.

Behind them, the earth was thundering.

Nine warriors. Nine horses. Men in dark indigo with white cowries stitched at the shoulder, quivers strapped across their backs, faces set like carved wood. They had been riding since the river crossing at dawn and they had not slowed once, not to drink, not to speak, not to let their animals breathe. They rode the way a fire crosses dry grass — without mercy, without hurry, without any thought of stopping.

The first arrow came so close to Yidana's ear that she felt the wind of it on her cheek.

"Down!" Asana shouted, and Yidana went flat against her horse.

Two more arrows. Three. They came in a loose, whistling rain, and the young women rode through it the way one rides through a storm — not dodging, exactly, but leaning, weaving, letting the horses swing wide and then wider, throwing themselves left when the sound came from the right. An arrow buried itself in the ground ahead of Asana and her mare jumped it clean. Another split the air between the two of them and went on into nothing.

"The gully!" Asana called. "Take the gully and they will have to ride single —"

She never finished.

Yidana heard it before she understood it: a sound like a hand slapping wet clay. Asana's whole body jerked forward over the horse's neck. For a moment she stayed there, folded, and then she came up again slowly, slowly, and Yidana saw the shaft standing out of her back below the left shoulder blade, quivering with the rhythm of the gallop.

"Asana —"

"Ride." Asana's voice had gone thin and strange. "Don't look at me. **Ride.**"

They rode.

Yidana would remember it afterward as the longest stretch of ground she ever crossed in her life, though it could not have been more than a few thousand paces. Asana stayed upright. Somehow, she stayed upright. Her hands were knotted in the mane, her head kept dropping and jerking back up, and a dark stain spread down the back of her wrapper and along the mare's flank in a widening streak, and still she stayed upright, and still the mare ran.

The grass thinned. The ground turned pale. And then the earth simply stopped being green and became sand — a great open sweep of it, ridged and rippled and glowing orange in the failing light, and the horses' hooves went from drumming to hissing as they hit it.

Asana slid sideways off the horse without a sound.

## II. The Cliff

She struck the sand shoulder-first and rolled.

Yidana hauled her mare around so hard the animal screamed, and by the time she had it turned, Asana was already going over the lip of the dune — not a dune, a cliff, the sand ended and dropped away into a long red gorge — and she was rolling down it, arms loose, legs loose, the arrow snapping off somewhere in the tumble, her body throwing up little bursts of sand each time it struck and turned and struck again.

Yidana did not think. She threw herself off the horse while it was still moving, hit the ground badly, got up anyway, and ran.

The slope took her feet out from under her within five steps. She went down on her side, slid, caught herself, half-ran and half-fell the rest of the way, sand pouring down around her in a hissing sheet, her palms burning, her heels digging trenches. Below her, Asana's body bounced once more against an outcrop of stone, spun, and finally came to rest at the very bottom — half in the shallow stream that ran along the floor of the gorge, one arm flung out across the wet gravel.

Yidana reached her and dropped to her knees in the water.

"Asana. Asana. Look at me. Look at me."

Asana's eyes were open. The water moved around her shoulders and ran clear and then not clear at all, curling away downstream in slow red threads. Her chest rose. It rose again. The sound it made was wrong.

Yidana took her sister's hands in both of hers. They were cold already, cold in the middle of a desert, and that was the thing that broke her.

"I'm going to lift you," she said. "I'm going to lift you and we're going to —"

"Go, my sister." Asana's voice was barely more than breath. "You know the mission is bigger than me."

"Don't. Don't say that to me."

Asana's free hand moved. It went under the folds of her wrapper, fumbling, and came out holding a book — a small thick thing bound in dark hide, its edges worn round with handling, its cover tied shut with a leather cord. It had been against her body the whole ride. It was dry. She had kept it dry.

She pushed it into Yidana's hands.

"Take it." Her fingers closed over Yidana's, pressing them shut around the binding. "Take the book to Jakpa. Let him know we sacrificed our lives." Her eyes found Yidana's and held them, and there was nothing soft in them at all, nothing but command. "Run, my sister. **Run.**"

Above them, at the top of the cliff, a horse whinnied.

Yidana looked up. Nine silhouettes had gathered on the rim against the burning sky, long and black and unmoving, looking down.

She looked back at Asana one last time. Then she stood, holding the book against her chest with both arms, and she left her sister lying there in the stream with the water moving over her, and she ran.

## III. The Forest

They came down the cliff on foot.

She heard it happen — the creak of leather, the thud of men dropping from their saddles, the long grinding rush of nine bodies coming down a slope of loose sand. She did not turn around. She ran along the stream bed with the gravel skidding under her and the book banging against her ribs, and when the gorge opened out she left the water and ran across open ground toward the dark line of trees on the far side.

They were fast. They were rested and they were fast and they were men who had been chosen for exactly this, and she could hear them gaining — not shouting, that was the terrible part, not one of them wasted breath on shouting — just the sound of feet, many feet, closing.

The trees took her in like a mouth closing.

Inside, the light went green and then grey. She ran with her arms up against the branches, through curtains of hanging vine, over roots that rose to trip her and did. She fell twice. She got up twice. Somewhere in there she lost one sandal and did not notice for a long while. The forest floor was soft and rotten and it swallowed her footsteps, but it swallowed theirs too, and now she could not tell how close they were, only that they were.

Her legs began to fail her.

It came on her all at once, the way it does — the burning in her thighs turning to water, her breath going ragged and useless, black spots blooming at the edges of everything. She could not keep running. She understood this with perfect clarity, the way one understands a fact about the weather. She could not keep running, and so she had to stop, and stopping meant choosing where.

She chose a nest of grey boulders leaning together beneath a strangler fig, their bases furred with moss, a low black gap beneath them barely wide enough for a body.

She went in on her belly, dragging the book, and pulled her legs in after her, and lay still.

The forest went quiet.

Then the feet came.

They came into the little clearing and they stopped, all of them at once, as if at a signal. Yidana lay in the dark under the rock with her cheek pressed to cold earth and looked out through a hole no wider than her hand. She could see boots. Nine pairs. They stood in a loose ring, turning slowly.

She was breathing too loudly. She knew it. She pressed her forearm across her mouth and it did not help; her whole body was one great bellows and it would not be quieted, and each breath came out of her in a shudder she was certain they could hear from where they stood.

A pair of boots came forward, out of the ring, into the center.

"We know you are here."

The leader's voice was calm. Almost kind. That was the worst of it — that a man could sound like that.

"Just hand over the book and we will spare your life." A pause. The boots turned, unhurried, one slow circle, taking in every shadow. "It doesn't have to be difficult. Unless you want it to be."

Yidana did not move. A beetle crawled over the back of her hand and she did not move.

They searched. It took a long time — long enough that her hands went numb where she gripped the book, long enough for the light through the canopy to shift and dim. She heard them beating the undergrowth with their bow staves. She heard one of them walk directly past the rocks, close enough that a spray of dirt from his heel came through the gap and landed on her lips. She did not close her eyes. She watched.

Then the leader spoke again, and his voice had gone flat and bored.

"Let's go."

The boots turned away. One by one they went — she counted them, she made herself count them — and the sound of them faded into the trees and thinned and finally was gone, and there was nothing left in the clearing but the ordinary noise of a forest at evening, insects starting up, something calling far away.

Yidana let out the breath she had been holding for what felt like her entire life.

She lay there a moment longer. Then she pushed the book out ahead of her and crawled out after it, into the grey air, and got her feet under her, and stood up on legs that shook. She put a hand against the rock. She was alive. She had the book. She would find Jakpa and she would tell him — she would tell him what Asana had said, exactly as she had said it, word for word —

Behind her, high up, someone whistled.

It was a short sound. Two notes. Almost casual.

Yidana froze.

Her hand was still on the rock. The book was still against her chest. Everything in the forest kept moving except her — the leaves, the insects, the last light going out of the canopy — and she stood absolutely still in the middle of it, and then, very slowly, she began to turn her head.

She looked over her shoulder.

The leader of the warriors was standing on top of the rock she had been hiding under, bow slack in one hand, looking down at her.

Yidana screamed.

# Part Two: The Crossing

## IV. The Rock

The scream tore out of her and was gone, swallowed by the trees, and the forest did not care.

The leader stood on the rock and watched her with the patience of a man who has already won. He did not raise the bow. He did not need to — because now, from between the trees on every side, the others were coming back. They had never left. They had walked away loudly, in a body, boots on dead leaves, and then they had come back around one soft step at a time and taken their places in the dark, and they had waited for her to climb out of her own grave and stand up in the middle of their circle.

Eight men stepped into the clearing. The ring closed.

"You did well," the leader said, and the awful thing was that he meant it. "Three days. Two rivers. My cousin's best horses." He crouched on the rock, forearms on his knees, and looked down at her almost gently. "It is finished now. Give me the book, child. Walk home. Mourn your sister. No one will follow you."

Yidana's arms tightened around the book.

Asana had kept it dry through an arrow and a cliff and a stream. Asana had held it against her body while her life ran out of her into the water, and her last strength on this earth had gone into pressing it shut inside Yidana's hands. There was no version of Yidana that opened those hands. She understood that about herself now, standing in the ring, the way you understand a thing only when it is tested: she was going to keep the book or she was going to die holding it, and those were the only two roads out of this clearing.

So she looked for a third one.

Her eyes moved without her head moving — Asana had taught her that, years ago, hunting guinea fowl in the grass: *look with your eyes, sister, your head is a flag.* The ring of men. The gaps between them, each one narrowing. The rock above her with the leader on it. And behind her, at her heels, the strangler fig — its host tree long dead and rotted out, its roots grown down around the hollow like the bars of a cage, and low in that cage of roots a gap. A small gap. A gap for a duiker, or a child.

Or a girl who had not eaten in two days.

The ground under it sloped away. And from somewhere down that slope, faint under the insect noise, she could hear what she had been too frightened to hear before: water. Big water. Not the stream. The stream's mother.

"Child," the leader said, and there was a first thread of iron coming into the kindness now. "Do not make me come down."

"All right," Yidana said.

She let her shoulders fall. She let the book sag in her arms, let it swing loose in one hand, held it a little away from her body — and she watched the whole ring of them do the thing that men do at the moment they believe a thing is over. Their weight settled back onto their heels. Their eyes went to the book instead of to her. And the leader, satisfied, stood up on the rock and stepped off it, and for the length of one breath he was in the air, and then for the length of another he was landing — knees bending, eyes down, hands out.

Yidana went through the fig.

She dropped and shot through the root-gap like a fish through a weir, the book scraping through ahead of her, bark tearing her shoulder, and she was out the other side and running down the slope before the shout went up behind her. She heard the ring break. She heard bodies hit the root cage and curse it — grown men, quivers and bows and shoulder-cowries, jamming in a gap that had barely passed a starved girl — and she heard the leader's voice crack out over all of it, no kindness left in it at all:

"**Around!** Go *around*, she is going to the gorge —"

He knew the country. Of course he knew the country. It did not matter. She had ten paces on them and the slope was hers, and she ran down it in the dark not like something fleeing but like something falling, the way Asana's body had gone down the sand — loose, surrendered, letting the mountain do the running — and the sound of the big water came up to meet her.

## V. The Crossing

The forest ended at the edge of the world.

She came out of the trees onto bare rock and the gorge was simply there, a black crack in the earth wider than a bowshot, and far below in the bottom of it the river went past in the dark with a sound like a granary burning. Spray stood in the air. The last light lay along the far rim, dull red, a coal about to go out.

And spanning the dark, sagging between two great posts of wood, was a bridge.

It was old. It was vine and twisted bark and plank, the kind the salt traders used in her grandmother's time, and half the planks were gone and the ropes were furred with moss, and Yidana looked at it and heard the feet coming down through the forest behind her and did not have the luxury of deciding whether she trusted it.

She pulled the leather cord from the book's binding, lashed the book flat against her breastbone, knotted the cord behind her back with fingers she could not feel, and stepped out over the dark.

The bridge moved under her like a living thing. It swayed and shuddered and dropped sickeningly at each step, and she went along it with a hand on each vine rail, sliding her feet, not looking down — looking down was the whole world, there was almost nothing *but* down — and she was a third of the way across when the warriors broke from the trees behind her, and half across when the first arrow went past her into the gorge with a sound like a struck harp string.

"**Hold!**"

The leader's voice. The arrows stopped.

"She carries it on her body," he said, somewhere back on the rock, calm again, terribly calm. "It goes where she goes. No one shoots. — Cut her off the far side. *You two, with me.*"

The bridge bucked. He had stepped onto it.

She felt him through the vines — his weight, and one man behind him, and another — three men coming over the dark on the same dying ropes, and the whole bridge groaning and sawing at its lashings, and Yidana stopped being careful. She ran. Planks turned under her feet and one went spinning away into the roar below and she ran over the hole it left, and the far post came up out of the dark and she threw herself the last body-length and landed on stone on her hands and knees.

The lashings were in front of her face.

Old vine, turned hard as horn, wound around the great post in ten strangling loops — but old, cracked, gaping. And wedged in the crack of the rock beside the post, where some trader had left it in her grandmother's time or God had put it there for her, a flake of black stone with an edge.

Yidana did not decide anything. Her hands decided.

She sawed. The first vine parted with a snap like a knucklebone. The bridge lurched. Out in the dark the three men stopped, and she heard the two behind begin to shuffle backward, fast, and she heard the leader do something much worse. He came on.

"Yidana." First time he had used her name. The bridge heaved under his coming, closer, closer. "You cut that, you kill me. You are not a killer of men, daughter of Gushegu. I have watched you three days. You dodge. You hide. You run." The vines shivered; his voice was almost at the post. "You do not kill."

The second vine parted. The third.

"Asana," Yidana said — to him, to the dark, to the river — and cut the last one.

The bridge went out from under the sky. It fell away sideways into the gorge with a long tearing shriek, planks scattering off it like seeds off a shaken head, and the leader of the warriors went down with it — down, and then not down: swinging, riding the dead bridge as it swung on its far lashings and smashed against the cliff below the other rim, and she saw him in the last red light, small and far away, clinging to the wreck of it above the water, alive. His two men were hauling themselves up the ruin toward the safe side. The other six stood on the far rim in a line, black against the coal-red sky, and not one of them could do anything at all.

The nearest ford was Kpandai. Two days' ride. She knew it, and every man on that rim knew it.

Yidana got up off her knees. Her hands were bleeding and would not stop shaking, and the book was hard and dry against her breastbone, and across the gorge the leader had climbed high enough on the wreckage to look at her. Just look. Then he freed one arm from the vines, slowly, and raised his bow over his head — not to draw it. A salute. Or an oath.

*This is not finished,* said the bow against the sky.

Yidana turned her back on all nine of them and walked into the trees.

## VI. Smoke

She walked all night, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the stream, and the water going red, and the cold hands.

She walked through the dark forest and out of it into open savanna under stars her grandmother had names for, and she said the message aloud to herself as she went, over and over, so that her feet had something to march to. *Take the book to Jakpa. Let him know we sacrificed our lives.* We, Asana had said. Not *I*. Even bleeding into a stream, Asana had already counted her sister's life as spent, as given, as part of the price — and Yidana walked under the stars and understood at last that she was not carrying the book *for* Asana. She was the second half of Asana's sentence. She was still being spent.

"Run, my sister," she said to the dark, in Asana's voice, and kept walking, because there was no run left in her, and walking was what she had.

Dawn found her on her feet.

It came up grey, then bone, then gold, and the land rose in front of her into a low line of hills with baobabs standing on them like old men who had stopped mid-journey to think. And beyond the hills, thin and straight in the still morning air, stood a column of smoke.

Not a burning-smoke. A cooking-smoke. A village-waking-up smoke, a smoke that meant millet porridge and dogs barking and old men sitting against warm walls — and Yidana stood swaying in the grass with her bleeding hands and the book cord biting her back, and she laughed, one broken syllable of it, the first sound she had made in hours.

Jakpa's village. It was real. It was there.

She was still laughing, or perhaps by now it was the other thing, when she started up the hill toward the smoke — one foot, then the other foot, the book knocking softly against her heart at every step like a second pulse, like the pulse of someone else carried out of a stream and over a gorge and across a night, all the way home.

Behind her, two days' ride away at the ford at Kpandai, nine horses were crossing the river.

**END OF PART TWO**
